How I turned thousands of Chatty users into actionable customer segments.

Chatty had just changed direction — from a simple FAQ tool into an AI live-chat app for online stores. The team had one basic question: who are our customers? We had 10,000 users but only ~250 paying, and no one could describe a typical merchant in a sentence. I built the first customer segmentation (a way to sort customers into a few clear groups), focused on the 250 paying merchants — the ones whose behaviour showed what they actually valued.

A classic model, rebuilt to fit us

RFM is a classic way to group customers by how recently, how often, and how much they buy. That works for a shop — but not for us: our merchants don't buy products, they set up and use the AI. So I kept RFM's simple three-part shape and swapped in three signals that actually matter for Chatty.

Training Depth — does the merchant teach the AI (add FAQs and answers), or leave it untrained? Login Frequency — how often they come back each week. Resolution Rate — how many chats the AI handles on its own, without a human stepping in. Three simple numbers any team can understand.

Why I kept it simple

A fancy clustering algorithm would have looked more sophisticated. I skipped it on purpose. The goal wasn't a perfect math score — it was one shared picture that Product, Support, and Marketing could all use. A model nobody can explain dies in a slide deck. So I optimised for the thing that actually matters: will people use it?

Three clear types emerged. I grouped merchants by behaviours the business already understood, then double-checked against store size, industry, and age — and the groups held up. Each type now had both a what they do and a who they are.

What it changed

The proof was adoption, not a metric. Product rebuilt onboarding around it: a new merchant's first screen now asks which type they are — AI-powered · Human–AI · Human-only — and each answer sets the app up differently, showing only the features that type needs. AI-powered merchants get the deep training tools; Human-only merchants get a simple chatbox with AI hidden.

Merchants reach what they need faster, instead of wading through features they'd never use. Support and Marketing picked up the same three labels too. When three teams adopt the same model at once, that's the validation that counts — it shaped what every new merchant sees on day one.

How I gave PMs full visibility into every dollar gained and lost.

Each month our MRR (monthly recurring revenue — the subscription income we can count on) went up or down, but the team could only see the final number, not why it changed. A single number is like a thermometer: it tells you the temperature is rising, not whether it's a fever or a hot kitchen. With thousands of payments flowing through every month, the only honest way to explain the change was to sort each payment by the reason behind it.

Two tables, one story

Our billing data lived in two places. One table logs every payment — charges, refunds, credits, one-time fees. The other logs each store's history — installed, subscribed, cancelled, paused. To tell whether a $29 charge is a brand-new customer or a returning one, you have to read that store's history before the payment.

The tricky part was linking the two. They only connect cleanly on one kind of row, so refunds and cancellations arrive with no link back to the original subscription. I had to fill that link in across each store's timeline to reconnect every event to the right charge.

Twelve reasons, not four

The standard playbook sorts revenue change into four buckets: New, Expansion, Contraction, and Churn (customers leaving). Our billing needed more. Annual plans bring in revenue in months with no charge. Refunds count as negative. Stores can freeze, unfreeze, and come back. One-time fees aren't recurring at all. Pay-as-you-go AI usage counts as recurring for Chatty but not for other apps. Once I mapped them all out, it came to twelve distinct reasons.

Twelve types — six that grow revenue, five that drain it, one that holds steady — with no overlaps and no gaps.

The monthly output

The result is one view PM reads every month: each dollar of revenue change, sorted into the twelve reasons. Gains stack above the line, losses below — so at a glance you can see what drove growth and what leaked.

How I uncovered the hidden driver behind Chatty's rising churn.

Through mid-2025, Chatty's churn (the rate at which paying merchants cancel) crept upward. The number was small — a couple of percent — but the trend pointed at roughly $3K of lost monthly revenue by year-end. PM didn't want a chart of the problem. PM wanted the cause — something Product could fix.

Turning a vague question into a measurable one

Why are merchants leaving? isn't something a model can answer directly. Which traits best predict who leaves? is. So I built a simple prediction model: I labelled each merchant as Churned or Active, gave the model nine traits about each one (their profile, how they set the app up, and how well the AI performed), and let it rank what mattered most.

What the model showed

Two signals stood out. Merchants who leaned on Scenarios to train the AI were the most likely to leave. Merchants who used FAQs and Q&A pairs were the most likely to stay. How well the AI resolved chats helped retention too; the rest barely mattered.

Same start, different endings

The model told me what mattered, not why. So I followed two groups over their first 180 days — those who trained the AI with Scenarios, and those who trained it with data (FAQs, Q&As, files). One signal told the story: how much of the chat the AI could handle on its own.

Both groups started the same: the AI handled ~80% of chats at first, then dropped to ~50% by Day 30 — the moment a merchant has to choose to train it better or live with the gap. From there they split. The data-trainers climbed back above 85%. The Scenario-trainers stayed stuck near 45%. That gap is what drove the cancellations.

The root cause: a misunderstanding

Once I separated the two groups, the cause was clear. Merchants brought a ChatGPT habit to Chatty — they used Scenarios as a place to dump knowledge. But Scenarios are for behaviour ("how the AI should respond"), not facts. So the AI never learned what they were trying to teach, its answers stayed weak, and they cancelled.

The merchants who used the right places — FAQs, Q&As, files — saw the AI improve, and they stayed.

Merchants used Scenarios like a ChatGPT prompt box. The problem wasn't a missing feature — it was the gap between what merchants expected and how the product actually worked.

What shipped

The fix turned the diagnosis into three moves: in-app guidance that explains Scenarios vs. data sources up front; easier paths to the data sources merchants kept reaching for (auto-imported FAQs, suggestions from their own store, unanswered questions pulled from past chats); and the same signals fed the Churn Alert System so Support could reach at-risk merchants early.

What the trend did next

The next quarter, churn reversed by about five points — roughly $3K of monthly revenue saved. With clearer in-app guidance and easier paths to the right data sources, more merchants trained the AI the way it actually learns. Resolution rates climbed, and fewer merchants cancelled.

How I helped merchants reach Chatty's aha moment faster.

PM asked the big question: how do we get more trial users to pay? Taken literally, that has a thousand answers. I narrowed it to one I could actually research — what makes a trial merchant decide to pay, and where do the rest get stuck? The goal was a roadmap, not just a number. That reframe is the real work.

Where I focused

First I split trial users by how they arrived. An onboarding-trial merchant started a paid trial right after signing up. A later-trial merchant used the free plan for a while — weeks or months — then upgraded on their own.

Two angles on the same users

To understand what drives trial conversion, I studied 1,660 onboarding-trial merchants from May 2025 to May 2026, focusing on their first 7 days of product usage. The analysis combined two perspectives: Customer Behavior — how merchants navigated Chatty during the trial, and Customer Voice — the questions, feedback, and pain points they shared with Support.

I grouped 1,660 merchants into 11 user segments based on product usage (4 payer groups, 7 non-payer groups), then analyzed their Support conversations. By matching payer and non-payer groups with similar goals, I uncovered three key barriers to conversion.

Three findings, true for every pair

Across all three pairs, the same three facts held — not about any one group, but about the path from sign-up to paying.

One: converters and non-converters share the same setup pattern across the app — they touch the same product areas, but only converters finish setup. Non-converters stall before ever reaching the parts that would have won them over (AI Training, Settings). Two: every cluster drops by half after Day 1. Merchants who don't feel progress in the first session treat the trial as wasted time and stop coming back. Three: the aha moment isn't in the product — it's in Support. About 85% of CS conversations end with the merchant saying "wow" once Support walks them through. Until they get stuck and contact CS, they don't understand what Chatty actually does for them.

The chain of cause and effect

The three findings collapse into one chain with two blocks — one before AI Training, one after. Block 1 (pre-AI): setup is split across Appearance (Chatbox + FAQs) and Settings (channel and app integration, handover rules) — both must finish before merchants reach AI Training. That takes 2–3 days, not the first session, so most merchants drop off before this gate clears. Block 2 (post-AI): even when merchants finish training the AI, they still don't know how to apply it — they have a business case but no clear sense of which Chatty features map to it. They get stuck and contact CS for help. The aha moment lands there — in the Support conversation, not in the product.

Three actions from the research

With the two blocks diagnosed, the team shipped three changes — two to close Block 1 (Setup Hurdle) so merchants reach AI Training in the first session, and one to start chipping at Block 2 (Usage Gap) so the aha moves out of CS chat and into the product.

What the fix moved

Product redesigned the chatbox and FAQ setup with one goal: finish it fast. Appearance is Priority 1 not because it matters most on its own, but because it's the gate to the part that does. Comparing merchants who onboarded before the redesign (Jan–Mar 2026) with those after (Apr–May 2026):

Time to finish AI setup fell from ~2–3 days to ~60 minutes — exactly the thing the redesign targeted. The downstream signal followed: across Apr 1 – May 31, the onboarding trial-to-paid rate climbed ~3 percentage points versus the Q1 baseline — early but directional. The measurement plan shipped with the roadmap so Product can keep tracking the lift over a longer window and confirm it holds.

How I turned Chatty's North Star into team-level ownership.

PM's diagnosis was sharp: "Chatty only has lagging metrics — the North Star and the headline rates. I need leading metrics: ones we can control now, act on quickly, and that actually move the big numbers." (A lagging metric reports what already happened; a leading one warns you in time to act.) Just as important: every team needs metrics they own — otherwise everyone chases revenue, which no one can move directly, and no one owns the levers that actually move it.

The frameworks behind it: MECE + logic tree

Designing a metrics ecosystem isn't a writing exercise — it's a decomposition problem. To make sure every metric that matters has a place (and nothing that matters is missing), I lean on two complementary frameworks: MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive) and the logic tree. MECE guards against double-counting and blind spots — at every layer, the children must partition the parent without overlap and without leftover. The logic tree forces decomposition top-down: start from the North Star, ask "what drives this?", break each driver into its own MECE children, and stop only when each leaf is something a real team can act on. This case study is how I applied both to design Chatty's six-layer metrics ecosystem.

Built from analysis, not a template

It's easy to prompt an AI for a metrics framework. The real skill is doing the analysis first to find which metrics actually matter. The framework comes out of the analysis, not the other way around.

Before defining a single metric, I ran the install-to-trial and trial-to-paid analyses (that work became How I helped merchants reach Chatty's aha moment faster) to learn what actually moves users forward. Then I built the framework up from the findings. Every metric had to earn its place: does it connect to the North Star, and does the data show it affects the level above it? No evidence, no slot.

Six layers, six audiences

Each layer is a MECE decomposition of the layer above — children partition the parent without overlap and without leftover — and each layer answers a different team's question. Layer 0 is the North Star: total revenue. Layer 1 breaks that into six plain growth-vs-loss buckets (rolled up from M1b's twelve). Layer 2 holds the three headline rates — install-to-trial, trial-to-paid, and churn — where PM lives. Layer 3 splits into a step-by-step conversion funnel and a set of churn warning signals. Layers 4–5 hold the detailed metrics that designers, support, and engineers actually touch.

Lagging at the top, leading at the bottom

The label only matters because of where in the tree the metric sits. Layers 0–2 are lagging — MRR, the growth/loss buckets, the three headline rates. They report what already happened, so they're for leadership to read, not for any one team to "move" directly. Layers 3–5 are leading — the funnel stages, the parallel signals, the team-owned levers. These tell you which merchants are stuck while you can still help them, and they're the metrics individual teams actually own.

Lead and lag, working together — one usecase

Example, straight from the tree. Chat-to-Sales Rate (L4 — % of shoppers who buy after talking to Chatty) drifts down. It's a roll-up; no team can move it directly. Drop to L5: the Chat-to-Sales Funnel breaks it into four leading stages — Click-through, Engagement, Add-to-Cart, Check-out. Three are flat, but Click-through Rate is down 5pp. Diagnosis is instant: Chatty is suggesting the wrong products. Data retunes the match algorithm; CTR recovers in two weeks; Chat-to-Sales Rate follows a month later. Without L5 under L4, the team would have hunted in checkout or pricing — when the break was at the very first click.

Who owns what

The framework's contract is a simple map of team → layer. Leadership reads Layers 0–2 (overall health). PMs read 2–3 (where to focus). Designers, BA, dev, and Support read 3–5 (the levers they control). Without the map, everyone tries to move the North Star — which isn't any one team's job to move directly.

How I proved Chatty drives revenue for merchants.

Chatty sells itself as a Sales AI — an assistant that helps online stores sell more. We did report a revenue number, but the logic behind it didn't convince merchants. The old rule was loose: when a shopper finished checkout, we checked whether they'd interacted with Chatty and whether Chatty had suggested one of the purchased products — if both were true, the whole order counted as Chatty-assisted. That's correlation dressed up as credit. I redesigned the attribution logic — and the data system behind it — to turn "trust us" into "here's the receipt." It builds directly on how I turned Chatty's North Star into team-level ownership: that framework's funnel and metric definitions are the foundation this attribution model is designed on.

From a black box to a clear rule

The old metric leaned on a flag in the database whose logic no one could trace. I replaced it with a journey we record step by step — so every sale credited to Chatty can be rebuilt from the shopper's own actions, from the first question to the final purchase.

The tables behind the number

The proof rests on one new table — end_customer_event — that records every step of the shopper's journey: the question, the click, the scroll, the add-to-cart, the purchase. It joins to the two conversation tables we already have on shared IDs. spans_v2 tells us whether a message actually fired the AI's product-search tool; chatbot_answers holds the question and the answer text. Together they let any credited sale be rebuilt end to end.

What it produces

The same event trail feeds two very different readers. For merchants, it's a dashboard: every action a single shopper took, the Assisted Revenue Chatty earned them, and the ROI on what they pay — all in one place. No black box. They can open any credited sale and see the exact steps behind it.

For the internal team, those same events line up into a sales funnel — the structure carried straight over from the North Star framework. The point isn't one number; it's that each rate isolates a different failure point, so a drop tells you exactly which part of Chatty's selling to fix.
